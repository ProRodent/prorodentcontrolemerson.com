Setting the stage for the walk-through.

Have a few details ready when you ring from Ho-Ho-Kus. They let the dispatcher match a technician and a time to the job.

  • Pets in the home, and whether any roam outdoors.
  • The location of the dryer vent, the furnace and the water heater.
  • The attic type: walk-up stairs, pull-down ladder or a small hatch.
  • If there is an attached or detached garage, and whether it has a loft or storage above.

What prompts a Ho-Ho-Kus call.

The first clue is rarely dramatic. Residents of Ho-Ho-Kus often mention one of the following:

  • Debris or droppings under the hood of a parked car.
  • Gnawed corners on cardboard boxes in the basement.
  • Droppings on a shelf in a pantry or storage room.
  • A smell that grows stronger when the heat runs.

A technician's route through the building.

Where a smell is the complaint, the search focuses on airflow, flies and warm patches. Once the source is found, the opening made is as small as possible.

Pictures are taken of whatever turns up and shared with you. They show the route in plain view, and they can help if an insurer asks about damage.

A house that has stood for decades may have more gaps than a recent build, yet newer homes often show gaps of their own around pipes, cables and vents that were cut in after the framing was finished.

Nothing about the work depends on guessing, because a technician reads the evidence in your building, follows it to the openings that animals use, and writes down both the findings and the plan so that nothing is left to memory.

It helps to keep a short note of what you notice before the visit, naming the room, the time of day and whether you hear scratching or smell something stale, because those details narrow the search much faster than a general description of the problem.

Dispatch opens with three questions: which part of the building, what hour, and what the noise sounds like.

Weather, months and rodent activity.

Summer storms can knock branches onto roofs, and a branch that touches the house is a bridge. Check the roofline after a major storm.

Mild winters shift the calendar. Without a hard cold snap, animals may come in later or stay outdoors longer, so the first-frost rule is only a guide.

Before the visit.

  • Chewed cable, nest scraps and pellets must stay out of reach of crawling babies and curious dogs.
  • Open a shed that has been shut for weeks and let it breathe before stepping in.
  • If you put a trap down already, let it stay put.
  • Mention recent roof work, gutter cleaning or leaf clearing when you call.

Cleaning in a safe order

Nothing here requires calling us first. A county health office would give you much the same directions for pellets found indoors.

  • A vacuum filter does not catch everything, which is why the machine stays out of this job.
  • Stale air in a shut-up crawl space or outbuilding should be replaced before you step in.
  • Hand protection comes first, and a soapy wash comes last.
  • Saturate droppings and stains with household disinfectant, or with bleach diluted as its label directs, then leave it alone for roughly five minutes.
  • Once the bag has gone in the bin, wash your hands again, even with clean gloves on.
  • After exposure, a cough, a fever or short breath is reason enough to ring a doctor soon.
