What one visit contains.

The technician begins outside and circles the house unhurriedly, studying eaves, vents, pipe collars, the footing and the garage door. Each gap and each trace of travel gets noted.

Seasonal patterns to know.

Snow cover can hide burrows and runways, but it also leaves tracks. A fresh snowfall is a good time to look for paths along walls and fences.

Chilly nights set things off. When the thermometer sinks, mice and rats start probing houses for warmth, and the first noises often follow within days.

Booking in practice.

Call from the property if you can. Sounds, smells and a quick look at the attic hatch make the conversation more specific.

If the problem is in a rental, tell the owner or manager. Repairs to the building usually need their approval, and shared walls make coordination worthwhile.

Readying the house.

  • Decant kibble and birdseed into containers with snap-on lids by dusk.
  • Lift the pet bowl at bedtime and wipe down the counters.
  • Hold off on your own traps; scattered ones blur the pattern a technician has to read.
  • Note the day the first sound, sighting or smell showed up.

Before touching any pellet

Most steps here are about stopping dust from drifting through the air.

  • Dust is the hazard, so the plan is simple: wet it down before anything gets lifted.
  • Open each outside door and window first, then give the room about thirty minutes.
  • Single-use gloves suit this job as long as you wash well after taking them off.
  • Soak the pellets and the ground around them, then leave the liquid alone for five minutes or so.
  • Double-bag anything wet, dripping or sharp before it goes in the bin.
  • Our expertise ends at the pest side, so anything about your body goes to a physician.
