How small a gap can be.

A mouse can enter through a seam about as tall as a pencil is thick. A rat needs a hole closer to the width of a thumb, but it will gnaw the edges until it fits. Neither takes long. A soft material, such as caulk alone, plastic or paper, gives way in a single night.

The rule we follow is simple: if you can fit a pencil in, it has to be closed, and it has to be closed with something harder than a rodent's teeth.

Where the openings are.

  • The foundation, where mortar has crumbled or a sill plate leaves a seam.
  • Utility entries: gas, water, cable, AC lines and electric service.
  • Vents for the dryer, bath, attic and crawl space.
  • The roofline, soffits, fascia returns and the places where roof meets wall.
  • Doors and windows, especially garage door corners and basement window wells.

Most houses show a pattern, with the biggest cluster at the lowest corner and the roofline. Where the house meets an addition, the seam deserves extra attention.

Order of work.

The sequence matters more than the materials. Sealing too early locks animals in, and they chew a fresh exit. So we trap first, let the catch fall, then seal in two passes: the obvious openings, then the ones the first pass revealed.

When there is doubt that every animal has left, we confirm before the last holes are sealed. The trap setting page describes how that tally works.

Houses that stand shoulder to shoulder.

Where houses stand close, a gap on a neighbor's wall can feed your side. You cannot fix their wall, but you can fix yours. Sealing your own foundation and roofline raises the odds that a problem next door stays there.

An annual recheck.

Walk the base of the house each autumn with a flashlight. Check that mesh has not shifted, vent screens are intact and sealant has not cracked. A ten-minute walk now is cheaper than a repair visit later, and you can call about anything that looks changed. For damage already done, see repairs after gnawing.

Small jobs for today.

  • Shut the noisiest room's door and leave it that way until a technician has looked around.
  • Leave dried droppings where they lie, since stirring them sends fine dust into the air.
  • Do not toss bait around, since an animal that eats it may retreat into the framing and die there.
  • Note which room and what time of each scratch, thump or sighting.

Dust and droppings: a safer way

Most steps here are about stopping dust from drifting through the air.

  • Never clean rodent waste dry. Brooms, dust mops and vacuum cleaners all throw invisible particles into the room.
  • A room that has sat sealed for weeks deserves a cross-breeze, so prop two openings and walk away for a while.
  • Keep a gloved hand away from your face while working, then wash both hands well.
  • Wet the waste thoroughly, wait the five minutes, then pick it up with paper towels.
  • Use tongs or a gloved hand to lift a carcass, and seal it in a bag at once.
  • Questions about a symptom belong with a medical professional, who can decide if rodents are involved.

What Emerson callers ask about sealing.

Why not use expanding foam?

Expanding foam is soft and gives way to teeth, and it bonds badly to dusty surfaces. Tucked behind metal it can serve as filler, but it must not be the sole barrier.

Might sealing shut an animal inside?

That risk is real, so trapping goes first. Sealing follows once the catch count hits zero, and the last openings close on the return visit.

How many entry points does a house have?

It varies widely. A small, tight house may have a handful. An older house with additions can have dozens. The walk-through produces a real count.

Does sealing alter how the house looks?

Most repairs are hidden behind trim, under eaves or at ground level. Where a vent screen or flashing is visible, we match color where we can.

Does sealing last forever?

Metal and cured sealant last a long time, but weather moves things. We recommend a quick look at the repairs each fall.
