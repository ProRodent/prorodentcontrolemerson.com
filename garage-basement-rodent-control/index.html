Why the lowest floor is the first to be hit.

Rodents arrive at ground level. A fieldmouse or a rat moving along a foundation tests every seam it meets, and a basement window well or a garage door corner presents plenty. Once an animal is inside the lowest level, it finds shelter, warmth from the furnace and a stash of stored goods.

That makes the basement and the garage the best places to read a problem. Droppings behind a water heater, gnawed boxes and tunnels through stored insulation tell a clear story, and the story usually ends at one or two openings.

What the check involves.

  • Walking the foundation from inside, looking for daylight, drafts and fresh droppings along the sill.
  • Examining each pipe, wire and duct where it passes through the wall.
  • Inspecting the garage door bottom seal and both lower corners.
  • Looking behind and under appliances, shelving and the workbench.
  • Opening a few stored boxes and bins to judge how deep any nesting goes.

A flashlight and a small mirror reach most places. In a finished basement, the check includes the drop ceiling and any access panels.

Garage doors need special attention.

The bottom seal on a garage door wears in a way that is easy to miss. Rubber hardens, corners curl, and a quarter-inch slot forms where the door meets the slab. A short rod of light shining through the gap at night tells the story. A new seal or a rebuilt threshold closes it.

While the door is open, look at the side jambs, the top corners and the point where the door track passes through the wall. Each of those is a favorite for mice. Sealing with steel and mesh explains the materials.

Cars in the garage.

A car parked for weeks can host a nest of its own. If you hear scratching from the engine bay, or find shredded material near the wheels, see engine nests and wire chewing. A garage that stays closed while the owners commute is just the kind of quiet space that attracts animals.

Keeping the space unattractive.

After trapping and sealing, small habits matter. Lift bins off the floor, keep a foot of clear space along the walls, store food in metal or heavy plastic and sweep the floor wet or with a damp mop. If droppings are present, follow the wet method in the CDC guide linked near the bottom of this page.

Before the visit.

  • Clear walking room beside the garage wall, under the sink and below the attic hatch.
  • Never shake out torn paper or cardboard; the particles travel.
  • Set any store-bought spring trap where kids and large dogs cannot reach it.
  • Point us to the room where the odor is strongest and we begin the search in it.

Small precautions before the visit

Treat this as a short guide to the first hour after you notice droppings.

  • Spritz dried waste with liquid first and only then touch it with a towel.
  • The airing period comes first and cannot be skipped, even when the job looks small.
  • Single-use gloves suit this job as long as you wash well after taking them off.
  • A generous soak, a short wait and a towel wipe, in that order, is the pattern the CDC lays out.
  • Use tongs or a gloved hand to lift a carcass, and seal it in a bag at once.
  • We do not diagnose illness. Anyone who feels sick after exposure should see a clinician.

What Emerson callers ask about basements and garages.

Why do garages attract mice?

Shelter, clutter and often a bag of pet food or seed. If the door seal has worn at the corners, a gap of a quarter inch is all they need.

Is a damp basement more of a draw?

Moisture can help, since animals need some water, but dry basements get visitors too. Warmth, food and quiet matter just as much.

Do I need to empty the space before you come?

No. A path to the walls is enough. Moving everything can scatter nests and droppings, so leave the contents in place unless we ask.

What about stored paper and fabric?

Cardboard and cloth make good nests. We suggest plastic bins with tight lids for anything you want to keep.

Are stone foundations harder?

They take longer, since mortar joints and irregular stone leave more seams. The work is the same in principle.
