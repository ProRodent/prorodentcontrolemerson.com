Why start with a look.

People often begin with a trap from the hardware store. Sometimes that works. More often it removes a single animal while the opening stays wide, and a month later the same sounds return. A walk-through reverses the order: find the route first, then decide what to place on it.

What the technician checks.

  • Eaves, ridge and gable louvers, and any spot where a limb touches the siding.
  • The foundation, sill plates, basement windows and each pipe or cable entry.
  • The attic, including insulation, wiring and the area around the hatch.
  • The garage door seal, its threshold and whatever is stacked against the walls.
  • The yard, sheds and fences, wherever burrows or runways might begin.

The technician carries a flashlight, a mirror and a measuring tape, and records photos of what is found. You are welcome to follow along. Many owners learn more in that hour about their own house than in years of living in it.

Reading what is found.

Pellets, gnaw marks, grease smears and nest scraps each tell part of the story. New pellets are dark and a little soft; old ones turn gray and fall apart. A rub mark that is shiny with grease means a route used often.

The written plan.

After the walk-through, you receive a plan that separates the work into stages. Trapping has a price, sealing has a price, and cleanout and repair, if needed, have their own. Nothing is bundled so tightly that you must accept it all.

The plan also includes follow-up dates. We would rather return on a set day and count what was caught than leave you guessing. If the findings show that the problem is small, we say so, and the plan reflects that.

Getting ready.

Make the attic hatch and the basement stairs easy to reach, and tell us about pets that may roam. Note when and where each sign appeared, and have any photos ready. If you have already used traps or bait, say where and how many.

Share your street and a detail or two, then dispatch hunts for the earliest slot.

Along busy roads such as Kinderkamack Road, one building can hold a shop below and a flat above. If that describes yours, mention both uses. We inspect each part and write the plan to match, and a business plan may fit the commercial side.

When the findings point to trapping, see trap setting and re-checks. If damage is already visible, repairs after gnawing explains that stage.

Before the visit.

  • If a car has leaves or fluff under the hood, stop driving it until someone has looked.
  • Bring bags of grass seed, birdseed and kibble inside, off the garage floor.
  • Seal any chewed item in a bag so the technician can study the bite.
  • Find the breaker panel ahead of time, in case a chewed wire turns up.

Dampen first, then clean

Treat this as a short guide to the first hour after you notice droppings.

  • Dry rags raise the same cloud a broom does, so keep them for other chores.
  • The airing period comes first and cannot be skipped, even when the job looks small.
  • Single-use gloves suit this job as long as you wash well after taking them off.
  • A generous soak, a short wait and a towel wipe, in that order, is the pattern the CDC lays out.
  • Wipe tools, mop heads and tabletop surfaces that touched the mess with the same disinfectant.
  • If you feel ill after handling droppings, tell your physician what you were cleaning.

What Emerson callers ask about inspections.

What does the technician look at?

The roofline, vents, foundation and every pipe or cable entry come first, followed by the attic, the basement or crawl space and the garage. The goal is to find evidence plus each opening in use.

Do I get the quote in writing?

Yes. The plan lists each task on its own line, whether trapping, sealing, cleanout or repair.

Can I book only the inspection?

You can. Some owners take the findings and decide later. The written notes are yours either way.

What is the length of a walk-through?

A typical house takes less than an hour, while a property with several outbuildings or a sprawling attic runs longer.

Should I clean up before the visit?

Please do not. Droppings and nest scraps are evidence, and sweeping them away raises dust. Clear the path to the attic hatch and basement stairs instead.
