Position beats quantity.

Rodents do not wander. A mouse follows the same few lines along walls and under shelving night after night, and a rat commits to a runway just as faithfully. A device placed on that line works within a night or two, while a dozen placed off it can sit untouched for a month.

That is why the walk-through comes first. Droppings, grease smears and tracks in dust mark the line, and we place devices at its corners, its entrances and the spot where it meets food.

We treat bait as the final option. Any station we place is locked, labeled and recorded in your plan, and the EPA page linked below explains how household products ended up in locked housings.

Why glue boards are out.

A glue board holds whatever steps on it. That includes birds, lizards, young squirrels and the neighbor's cat. An animal that is stuck but alive can scream and thrash for hours, and the remains are messy to dispose of. Snap traps work quickly and are cleaner to handle.

The re-check schedule.

Traps left alone drift out of date. A re-check lets us count the catch, replace spent devices and move the ones that saw no action. We write the return date on your plan before leaving, so you know when to expect us.

  • First return a few days after setting, when most of the activity shows up.
  • A second return if the count is still falling.
  • A final visit after sealing is complete, to confirm the quiet holds.

What you can do between visits.

Leave the traps where they are, even if you are curious about an empty one. Keep the doors closed on rooms where we worked and let us know if a pet found a way in. Tidy food away at night, as before.

If you notice a smell that was not there before, mention it at once. It can mean a catch behind a wall, and carcass pickup and odor tracing handles that case.

Once nothing more is caught.

Trapping alone leaves the door open. When the count reaches zero, the openings must be closed, or new animals walk in. Sealing with steel and mesh is the next stage, and a follow-up cleanout may be needed for soiled insulation.

Readying the house.

  • Keep the broom and the shop vacuum out of any room holding droppings.
  • Wait to open the bleach until we know how far the mess extends.
  • Set a quarter beside each gnaw mark so a photo shows its size.
  • Guess how long the signs have been appearing.

Small precautions before the visit

You may tidy up before the technician comes, as long as you follow the order below and pause whenever a step confuses you.

  • Dry rags raise the same cloud a broom does, so keep them for other chores.
  • Sheds, cabins and garages that were shut for a long time want half an hour of fresh air before anyone works inside.
  • Keep your hands covered for the entire job and wash them with soap and water when the gloves come off.
  • Saturate droppings and stains with household disinfectant, or with bleach diluted as its label directs, then leave it alone for roughly five minutes.
  • Double-bag anything wet, dripping or sharp before it goes in the bin.
  • When you see a doctor about any illness, mention the cleanup, since the clinician needs that detail.

What Emerson callers ask about traps.

Why not just buy traps at a hardware store?

You can, and a single mouse may be caught that way. The trouble is placement and count. A handful of traps in the wrong places teach the survivors to avoid them.

Do you use glue boards?

No. They also catch songbirds, chipmunks and the occasional pet, and the captive animal suffers for hours. Spring traps and enclosed stations are what we normally use.

Are traps dangerous for children or dogs?

Any trap needs care. Ours are placed in voids, behind appliances or inside covered stations, and we tell you where each one is so nobody is surprised.

Will one trapping visit be enough?

Usually there are two or three visits, scaling with the number of animals, and the plan names the return dates at the start.

What happens to the animals we catch?

They are bagged and removed from the property by the technician. You never have to handle them.
