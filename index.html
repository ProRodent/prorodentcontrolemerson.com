Emerson covers about two and a half square miles of Bergen County, and the borough calls itself The Family Town. Kinderkamack Road, which is County Route 503, passes through it. Wherever houses stand close together, a foundation gap on one lot is seldom far from the next lot's shed.

Wondering if rodents live nearby tells you little, since in any suburb they do. What matters is how easily your own house lets them in, and one unhurried walk around the footing with a flashlight beats guessing over coffee.

Telling a mouse job from a rat job.

Hold a pencil against a crack. If it fits, a mouse can follow it. A rat needs something nearer half an inch, though it gnaws to widen whatever it finds. Norway rats stay low and dig beside slabs, steps and stacked stone, while a roof rat, if one ever turns up, travels along wires and branches.

The type of trap, the weight of mesh and the cleanup approach all depend on which animal it is. So the species is identified on site before a price is written. Your phone description is a reasonable starting guess, and what we find confirms it.

What sends them indoors.

Across northern New Jersey the classic trigger is cold, although a downpour, a thaw or noisy construction next door can also set animals moving. A warm, dry house may be the best shelter for blocks around. Once the first hard frost has passed, walk the lot and look for new holes, displaced vent covers and leaves drifted against the siding.

  • Give each vent cap and pipe sleeve a firm wiggle in autumn, and look along the base of the garage door at both ends.
  • Move seed, kibble and lawn food into tubs with tight lids.
  • Stack firewood on a rack, away from the wall.
  • Rake leaves back and trim shrubs so a forearm of bare ground runs along the foundation.

Until we arrive.

A few quiet moves protect both people and evidence. Leave droppings unswept, send children and pets to a different room, and if you already set a trap, let it stay where it is.

  • Snap pellets and chewed spots with a quarter placed next to them so the size is clear.
  • Jot the hour and room of each noise.
  • Shut the door of the busiest room.
  • Avoid loose poison and glue boards; each brings a new set of problems.

Four stages from first look to final check.

Whether one mouse has found a cupboard or a colony holds a cellar wall, the work follows this sequence.

  1. 1

    Look.

    We start where the evidence is freshest, be it attic, basement, garage or yard, then circle the exterior hunting for droppings and the openings animals actually use.

  2. 2

    Catch.

    Each trap suits the species, follows the travel lines the evidence shows, and sits where children and pets will not reach it.

  3. 3

    Close.

    Holes, louvers and nibbled corners are closed with sheet metal or steel mesh, or filled with a compound that sets harder than a tooth can cut.

  4. 4

    Confirm.

    A second visit empties the traps, tugs on each repair and books whatever cleanup or restoration is still outstanding.

Clues that animals are living with you.

Finding a couple of these together in one place suggests current residents, not an old episode.

  • Droppings.

    Dark pellets near the size of a rice grain, spotted at the foot of walls, under sinks, on storage shelves or across attic insulation.

  • Recent chewing.

    Wood bitten this week is light-colored and ragged at the edge; look at joists, pipes, cable jackets and cardboard flaps.

  • Sounds after dark.

    A scratch, patter or dull thump inside a wall or ceiling, most often heard once the house goes still.

  • Bedding.

    A heap of torn paper, tugged-out insulation, cloth bits or dry leaves in a rarely visited corner.

  • Grease trails.

    Dark, oily smudges on walls, pipes and joists mark a route traveled every night.

  • A strange smell.

    A biting ammonia whiff in a shut room, or a heavy, sweet rot that hints at an animal that died nearby.

Two or three of these in one corner usually means live animals. Call before they chew any further.

Emerson landmarks and what they mean for a job.

Emerson station on the Pascack Valley Line.

The NJ Transit station in Emerson sits at Kinderkamack Road and Linwood Avenue, and trains first reached the borough in 1870. Some callers name the station to show where they live. That says nothing about your walls, so add your street and the age and style of the house, since an older building has more seams to examine.

Kinderkamack Road.

County Route 503 runs through the borough, and businesses may sit along it as they do on most county roads. If you own a storefront or a mixed-use building, say so when you call. A business with a back door and a shared dumpster gets a different plan from a single-family home.

The reservoir buffer and wooded edges.

Emerson lies near the Oradell Reservoir, and land around a reservoir is often left wooded, though we cannot say which lots touch it. If your yard backs onto trees, tell us. A yard that borders woods changes where the outside lap begins, whether or not rodents are involved.

What the setting changes.

Knowing a landmark lets a dispatcher picture your neighborhood, but it says nothing about your attic. A house on a dead-end street, a garden beside a hedge and a flat over a store would each send the visit somewhere different.

On narrow lots with houses close together, an overlooked gap is possible but far from certain. Walk the base of each wall with a flashlight, then call with what turns up.

Weekday-empty houses and quiet rooms.

Because the Pascack Valley Line serves the borough, some households leave early for the train, and a house that sits quiet for hours appeals to a mouse. Should that fit yours, spend part of a weekend looking over the pantry, the cellar and storage corners, then glance into the attic after any trip away.

Short answers before you dial.

Will a person pick up at 2 a.m.?

Yes, on every night of the year including holidays. When the board has room you are offered that very day, and if not, the next opening.

How can I tell mouse pellets from rat pellets?

Mouse pellets are about as big as a grain of rice. Rat droppings run larger and blunter. Heavy thumping overhead hints at something bigger than a mouse, and the technician makes the call on site.

Should I set out poison while I wait?

Seldom wise as a first step. An animal that eats poison may retreat into a wall and die there, and the smell can end up worse than the scratching ever was. The legal limits are set out on the EPA page linked near the end.
