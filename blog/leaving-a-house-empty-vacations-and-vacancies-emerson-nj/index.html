Leaving a House Empty: A Rodent Checklist for Vacations, Vacancies and Second Homes

Saturdays, Sundays and holidays reach the same staffed line as any Tuesday..

A house that sits empty for a few weeks becomes a quiet, undisturbed space. Rodents notice. A short checklist before you go, and a quick look when you return, catches most problems early.

People who travel for work, spend winters elsewhere or own a seasonal property often report the same pattern: they come back and something has moved in. The house was not damaged on purpose. It was empty, quiet and warm, and an animal found a way in.

What changes when no one is home.

Rodents avoid people and the vibrations they make. A house with no footsteps, no running water and no lights is a safer place to explore. Over a week or two, an animal that tested the foundation can move in, nest and breed before anyone notices.

The longer a house sits, the larger the problem tends to be when you return. A fortnight may mean a few droppings. A season can mean a nest in the attic insulation.

Before you leave.

  • Walk the outside and close gaps at the foundation, garage door and vents.
  • Put all food in sealed containers, including pet food and pantry staples.
  • Empty and clean the refrigerator if it will be off.
  • Take out the trash and recycling, and leave the bins closed.
  • Clear clutter near walls and keep stored items in lidded bins.
  • Close the damper or cap the chimney, and check that the attic hatch fits tightly.

If you can, ask a neighbor to check inside and outside once a week. A simple log of the date and what they saw is enough.

While you are away.

When you return.

Open the door and breathe. Notice any smell, then walk through the house before unpacking. Check the kitchen, pantry, basement, attic hatch and garage. If you see droppings, do not sweep them. Wear gloves, open windows and follow the damp method in the CDC guide linked below.

  • Take pictures of what you find, a quarter alongside for size.
  • Note the rooms and the amount of activity.
  • Check the car's hood before driving.
  • Call with your findings before you begin cleanup of large areas.

You need not know in advance whether it is a mouse or a rat; describe the signs and we narrow the field.

Empty rental units and vacant buildings.

Vacant units need a check between tenants. Look at kitchens, bathrooms, under sinks and around pipes. If you find activity, trap first, then seal, then clean and repair. Commercial and multi-unit plans can cover a building as a whole.

Seasonal and second homes.

Houses that sit for months face a longer period of risk. A pre-season check helps. Walk the outside, test the foundation and vents and book a walk-through before the family arrives. A house closed for the winter is a good candidate for a one-time sealing job that holds for years.

Cleanup afterward.

If animals nested, plan the stages in order: trapping, sealing, cleanout and then repairs. The attic and crawl space cleanout page describes the sequence. Rushing to replace insulation before the animals are gone only repeats the cost.

Before touching any pellet

The goal of these steps is to keep rodent dust from rising until help is on the way.

  • Sweeping is the habit to drop first: dried droppings crumble, and the powder is easy to inhale.
  • Sheds, cabins and garages that were shut for a long time want half an hour of fresh air before anyone works inside.
  • Keep your hands covered for the entire job and wash them with soap and water when the gloves come off.
  • The surface should look drenched rather than damp before you start wiping.
  • Wipe tools, mop heads and tabletop surfaces that touched the mess with the same disinfectant.
  • After exposure, a cough, a fever or short breath is reason enough to ring a doctor soon.

Straight answers.

How long is too long to leave a house?

There is no safe number, but a week or two is enough for animals to explore, and longer gives them time to settle. A check on return catches problems.

Should I ask a neighbor to look in?

A neighbor or friend who checks inside every week or two can notice signs early. Share what to look for and how to reach you.

What about an empty rental unit?

The same rules apply. Vacant units should be checked between tenants, and any gaps closed before new tenants arrive.

Do I need to turn off the heat?

Not for rodent reasons. Heat affects pipes. A warm house is more attractive to animals in winter, which is another reason to seal gaps.

What do I do if I return to a smell?

Open windows, find the strongest spot and call. See the guide on tracing smells. Do not sweep droppings dry.
