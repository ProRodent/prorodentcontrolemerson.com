Walking the Foundation: A Foot-by-Foot Guide to Where Mice Get In

Saturdays, Sundays and holidays reach the same staffed line as any Tuesday..

Most entry points can be found in an hour with a flashlight and a pencil. This guide walks you around the outside of a house, from the ground up, and shows what to look for at each stop.

Most of us imagine a mouse slipping in through a crack in a wall. In practice, the route is often a plain, unremarkable opening that has been there for years: a pipe collar that never fit, a vent screen that rusted, a garage door seal that curled. You can find these on a single walk.

Before you start.

Choose a dry day when the light is good, though you can walk at dusk with a flashlight to spot daylight at the base of walls. Bring a pencil. If it fits into a gap, a mouse may be able to follow. Note each opening on a rough sketch of the house.

Stop 1: ground level.

  • The foundation top, where mortar meets siding or sill plate.
  • Cracks in the foundation wall, especially at corners.
  • Basement window wells and the frames of small windows.
  • Gaps beneath steps, porches and the edge of a slab.

The lowest stop yields the most findings, since animals meet it first. Probe mortar joints gently with a screwdriver. Crumbly mortar is a flag.

Stop 2: utilities.

Look for every place a pipe, wire or cable passes through the wall. This includes gas, water, electric service, air conditioner lines, cable and satellite wiring. The opening around the line is rarely tight.

Stop 3: doors and windows.

Check the bottom of every exterior door. A door sweep that does not touch the threshold, or a weatherstrip that has worn away, leaves a slot. Hold a flashlight inside at night and look from outside for light. Garage doors are the most common weak spot, because the rubber ages and the corners lift.

Stop 4: the roofline.

Binoculars help from the ground. Look at the soffits, where the roof overhang meets the wall, and at the fascia boards, the gable vents and the ridge. Gaps where two roof sections meet are common. Branches touching the roof are bridges.

Rooflines are high and often unsafe for amateurs. If you see signs of chewing or gaps, photograph them and ask a technician to inspect. See sealing with steel and mesh for how we close them.

What materials hold and what to skip.

  • Holds: stainless or galvanized steel mesh, hardware cloth, sheet metal flashing, masonry patch compound, cured sealant.
  • Fails: foam alone, caulk alone, plastic, rags, paper, wood filler.

Steel wool is sometimes recommended, but animals can pull it out, and it rusts. If you use it, treat it as a backing behind metal and sealant.

Different houses, different weak points.

Houses here went up over many decades, so their weak spots differ. Old footings may show crumbling mortar or fieldstone with wide joints. Additions and dormers leave seams where new framing joins old. Newer homes can have gaps beside pipes and cables that nobody filled. Each house earns its own list.

After the walk.

Rank your findings by size and height. Close the low, large ones first, with a short list for a weekend. Larger projects can be added to a plan after a walk-through and quote. If animals are already inside, book trapping first.

Walk the house each year in autumn, and repeat after any construction, storm or roof work.

Small precautions before the visit

You may tidy up before the technician comes, as long as you follow the order below and pause whenever a step confuses you.

  • Dry rags raise the same cloud a broom does, so keep them for other chores.
  • CDC guidance asks you to ventilate a closed area before cleaning, and a half hour is the figure it gives.
  • Keep your hands covered for the entire job and wash them with soap and water when the gloves come off.
  • Wetting everything down keeps dust grounded and gives the disinfectant time to act.
  • Used towels, droppings and any dead rodent all go into one plastic bag, which you knot and put out with the weekly trash.
  • When you see a doctor about any illness, mention the cleanup, since the clinician needs that detail.

Still unsure?

Do I need special tools?

Bring a flashlight, a pencil for gauging gap widths, a notepad and a camera. A small mirror reaches awkward corners, and gloves help with debris.

What if I find a gap but no animals?

Seal it anyway. A gap is an invitation, and closing it before animals arrive is cheaper than closing it afterward.

Which gaps are most often missed?

The top of the foundation where siding ends, the place a cable enters through the wall, and the corners of a garage door.

Can I do all of this myself?

Many repairs are simple. Larger or high ones, such as soffits and rooflines, are better left to a technician with the right gear.

Seal first or trap first?

Trap first. If animals are still indoors, sealing can shut them in and cause fresh damage, and the re-check visit confirms the count.
